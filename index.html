How GPS Measures Distance
1. Time the signal

The radio signal leaves the city and arrives at each satellite a few thousandths of a second later. The clock measures that travel time.

2. Convert to distance

Radio waves travel at the speed of light, 299 792 km every second. Multiply the travel time by that speed to get the distance.

3. Plot circles

One measurement only says the city is somewhere on the perimeter of a circle around that satellite's location. We need to look for the intersection of three circles to specify a point.

4. Find the crossing

Your location is where all three circles meet. Imperfect clocks result in an inaccurate circle radius, identifying the wrong location.

Simulator limitations and simplifications
Flat map, not a globe

Real GPS satellites orbit about 20,200 km in medium-earth orbit, so each distance defines a sphere, not a circle, and signals take about 67 ms to travel from Earth to space. Here the satellites sit flat on the map and distances are measured in two dimensions. In real 3D-space, GPS needs four satellites.

The local clock

In real GPS the satellites carry the atomic clocks and only send signals; your phone just listens and times their arrival with cheap quartz. Its clock error is constant and adds the same amount to every distance, so it becomes just one more unknown to solve for. Set Perfect Phone Clock to False to see it. On a flat map three satellites can just barely pin down position plus clock, but there can be two possible answers; a fourth makes the answer unique and the math simple.

Relativity

GPS clocks run about 38 µs per day fast compared with clocks on the ground, because of their speed and weaker gravity. Left uncorrected, that would add roughly 11 km of error every day. Thankfully, this can be modelled using special relativity and corrected for, but is not captured in this simulator.

Satellite geometry

You can observe that the same clock error can move your estimated location by a little or a lot, depending on where the satellites are. When the circles cross at a shallow angle, small errors slide the crossing a long distance. Drag the satellites in Expectation mode to watch the region stretch. GPS engineers call this dilution of precision.
